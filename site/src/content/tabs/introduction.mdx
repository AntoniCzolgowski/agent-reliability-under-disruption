A familiar route can become useless overnight. The train stops running, a workplace closes, or an emergency changes where it is safe to go. Someone who has followed the same routine for years suddenly has to make different decisions. They may still have the same responsibilities, but circumstances differ. Other people are adjusting at the same time, often with incomplete information about what is happening. Their choices effect which roads become crowded and where help is needed. A decision that seems sensible in the morning may no longer work by the afternoon. Understanding this process means looking beyond where people usually go. It means asking how they respond when familiar expectations stop being useful. That question is at the centre of understanding human behaviour under disruption.

Everyday life provides a starting point because much of it repeats. People return to familiar places and arrange their days around routines and habits. This routines help households organise their lives and allow cities to plan services. In foundational research, Marta González, César Hidalgo and Albert-László Barabási showed that individual movement has strong regularities in both time and space ([González et al., 2008](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature06958)). Even across very different lives, returning to previously visited places is a common behaviour. This gives researchers a basis for understanding what an ordinary day might look like. However, a repeated action does not tell us everything about why it happens. Taking the same bus each morning could at the same time: reflect a preference or an obligation. Or in different case, the absence of an alternative. Those explanations may look identical while the bus is running, but when it stops, the details start play a role.

Human behaviour develops through the relationship between a person and their surroundings. A station matters because of the journeys it makes possible. A school shapes the daily movements of children and the adults responsible for them. A cafe might be someone's workplace, another person's meeting place, and a convenient stop for somebody passing through. The same location can therefore have very different meanings in different lives. Research by Andreas Züfle and collaborators connects urban life with people's needs and their social relationships ([Züfle et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10588-021-09348-7)). This perspective helps explain why knowing where someone is does not necessarily tell us what they are doing. It also brings attention to the choices that the surrounding city makes possible. If a familiar destination becomes unavailable, another nearby place may not serve the same purpose. Understanding it, requires paying attention to what people are trying to accomplish as well as where they move.

Disruptions make those complexe relationships easier to notice because they interrupt arrangements that usually remain in the background. A cancelled journey can affect a work shift, which can then affect a household's income. A workplace closure can change activity around nearby shops even when those shops remain open. Yabe and colleagues showed how changes in people's visits connect the fortunes of businesses across a city, allowing disruption in one place to affect others ([Yabe et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-024-02072-7)). Related work explored whether movement during rare events in one city could help anticipate how people might move in another ([Pang et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1145/3397536.3422244)).This connects the immediate question of where people go with the longer question of how communities recover. Recovery can involve returning to familiar routines, but it can also involve establishing different ones. A reopened building does not necessarily mean that the people who used it have returned. Some may have found alternatives, while others may still face obstacles.&#x20;

The ability to adapt is also unevenly distributed. Someone who can work from home faces different choices from someone whose income depends on being physically present. A household with access to a car have options that may be unavailable to a household dependent on one bus route. Caring responsibilities can make leaving an affected area difficult even when a person understands the danger. During the COVID-19 pandemic, Serina Chang and collaborators connected unequal exposure to differences in people's ability to reduce movement and in the places they visited ([Chang et al., 2021](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-020-2923-3)). Their work brings a public health perspective to the relationship between everyday activity and disadvantage. It also shows why an average description of a city can leave important differences out of view. Two people receiving the same advice may have very different opportunities to follow it.&#x20;

People also adapt in response to one another. A quiet route will become crowded once enough people decide to use it. Waiting may help one person avoid congestion while leaving someone else unable to reach an important appointment. These situations connect individual decisions with outcomes that nobody controls alone. Shirado and Christakis showed that introducing a little unpredictability into a group's interactions could help people escape coordination problems ([Shirado and Christakis, 2017](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature22332)). In transportation, Jang, Lichtlé, Vinitsky and colleagues investigated how automated vehicles could reduce repeated braking and acceleration, helping traffic move more smoothly ([Jang et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.17050)). Both studies illustrate how changing the behaviour of some participants can affect the wider group.

These questions have also motivated interest in digital stand-ins for real people, called in this research agents. Such a agent, represents a person within a computer-created setting. The broader ambition is to explore how people might respond to circumstances that would be difficult to examine directly. Work by Joon Sung Park, Michael Bernstein and collaborators has investigated how faithfully these stand-ins can reflect individuals' attitudes and choices ([Park et al., 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.10109v3)). This research brings attention to the difference between representing a broad social group and representing a particular person. People who share an age or occupation can still have very different priorities. Their experiences may shape what they notice, which advice they trust. On the other hand, responsibilities can influence what they are willing or able to do. A convincing account of a person therefore needs room for differences that broad labels cannot capture and a room for that person to change.

Change creates a difficult question about how long an understanding of someone remains useful. Knowing a person's ordinary routine may help explain what they usually do. It does not automatically tell us how they will respond when the circumstances supporting that routine disappear. An agent could continue behaving as though the familiar world were still available. Its actions might look reasonable while becoming increasingly disconnected from the person it represents. **This makes the difference between believable behaviour and faithful behaviour especially important.** A believable decision is one that somebody might make. A faithful description needs to remain connected to the particular person and situation it is meant to represent. The problem becomes more demanding when one mistaken expectation shapes several later ones. Reliability therefore concerns how well an understanding holds up as events unfold.

To keep this knowledge up to date, one need to recognize which changes are significant. One unusual journey may be a temporary exception. Several unfamiliar journeys could suggest a new routine, although the reason may still be unclear. Earlier knowledge remains useful, but recent experience may reveal that some of it needs reconsidering. This creates a tension between holding on to what was previously reliable and responding to what has just happened. Changing an expectation too quickly can mistake a brief interruption for a lasting shift. Changing it too slowly can leave an increasingly outdated picture of someone's life. There is also a difference between noticing that behaviour changed and understanding why it changed. A person may take another route because they learned something new, because their priorities changed, or because the original route became impossible. Research on adaptation needs to take that uncertainty seriously while asking what would make an account of the person useful again.

The motivation for this project is to understand when knowledge of ordinary behaviour remains dependable after ordinary conditions end. Cities provide a concrete setting because personal choices have visible consequences for shared places and services. Better understanding of it could help planners recognise where familiar assumptions are becoming outdated. It could also help researchers identify whose behaviour is being represented poorly and under what circumstances. Existing work has established important connections between routines, urban surroundings and collective outcomes. Further work can examine how those connections hold up when people face unfamiliar constraints. A central question is what fresh understanding is needed as people adapt and their choices affect one another. The aim is to understand how much we can trust our picture of human behaviour when the world around it changes.&#x20;

## Questions

1. What does a city look like during an emergency compared with a normal week: where do people stop going, and where do they go instead?
2. Are people who are seen less often during a disruption really moving less, or are they simply less visible to whoever is recording them?
3. Do people fall into recognisable kinds of movers, such as regular commuters and people who roam widely, and do some kinds keep their patterns in a disruption while others scatter?
4. How few traits are enough to tell one person's routine from another's?
5. Can the kind of place someone is at, such as a station, a shop, a school or a park, be guessed from where and when they are seen, without anyone saying so?
6. From what a person has just done, can one tell whether they are about to move on or stay put, and how far the next move will take them?
7. Does knowing what kinds of places surround a person help describe their ordinary days, and does that knowledge still help once routines break?
8. Researchers now build simulated cities out of agents, each one modelled on a real person. How faithfully can such an agent, built only from that person's everyday routine, describe them on an ordinary day?
9. When an emergency interrupts daily life, how quickly does the agent stop being a trustworthy description of its person, and did its record in normal times give any warning?
10. How much fresh evidence from the days after the disruption does the agent need before it is reliable again, and does it matter how it takes that evidence in or only how much of it there is?
11. In a simulation an agent has to keep going by itself, with nobody correcting it along the way. How long can it run before it drifts from what its person really did, and does it drift sooner during an emergency?
12. An agent goes out of date as its person's life moves on, but updating it is computationally expensive, so it can only be done a few times. Can it learn, from ordinary weeks alone, to notice when it is getting its person wrong and update itself in time? And does that still work the first time an emergency arrives?
