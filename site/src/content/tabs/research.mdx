## Invisible breaks

The [NaiveBayes](../naivebayes/) tab built an alarm for every person. Each day it adds up how much that person's day looks like the emergency, and it fires when the total crosses a line set so that only 5% of people would trigger it in ordinary times. It caught about a third of the routines that broke, most of them among people with regular weeks. A routine counts as broken when the person's **habit model**, a description of where they usually are at each half hour learned from their ordinary days, became far more surprised by their days in the emergency than chance allows. This finding asks about the breaks the alarm missed: whose broken routine an alarm can see, why some breaks stay invisible, what a fairer alarm changes and what no alarm of this kind can fix.

The political scientist James C. Scott argued in *Seeing Like a State* (1998) that a state sees best what it has made legible, the lives that fit its grid. An alarm built on the average person is such a grid. The analysis uses the 7,135 evaluation people of the course tabs, people who were never used to fit anything, sorted into five equal groups, or fifths, by **regularity**: the share of workdays on which they spent the middle of the day at their usual daytime place.

### Everyone's routine can break, but the alarm sees mostly the regular

Routines broke in every fifth: in 13.0% of the least regular people and 30.0% of the most regular. The alarm saw very different shares of those breaks (Figure 1). It flagged 4.3% of the breaks in the least regular fifth, then 17.4%, 24.6% and 44.8%, and 59.5% in the most regular fifth. By kind of mover it saw 75.5% of weekend movers' breaks and 64.1% of regular commuters', but 7.7% of mid-range movers' and 6.5% of far roamers'. Most breaks among people who live irregularly happened without the alarm ever firing.

### The same break, a quieter signal

Irregular people's breaks are not smaller. Measured by the habit model, the typical rise in surprise is 0.31 in the least regular fifth and 0.37 in the most regular (Figure 2, left). What differs is how far a break moves the daily evidence the alarm adds up, compared with how much that evidence wobbles on ordinary days. The ratio of the two is called d′ (d-prime), a measure from signal detection theory of how far a signal stands out from noise. For people whose routine broke, the typical d′ is 0.46 in the least regular fifth and 1.06 in the most regular (centre).

From d′ follows how many days of evidence it takes to tell a shift of that size from ordinary wobble, accepting a 5% chance of a false alarm and aiming to catch 80% of real shifts: about 29 days in the least regular fifth, 42 in the second, 25 in the third, 12 in the fourth and 5.5 in the most regular (right). The emergency lasted 15 days. Only 40% of the breaks in the least regular fifth could have become visible within it, against 71% in the most regular. And for more than a quarter of the breaks in each of the three least regular fifths, d′ is zero or below: the break did not move the evidence toward the emergency at all, so no amount of waiting would reveal it to this alarm.

### A fairer alarm

Part of the blindness comes from the alarm's design. It measures everyone on one scale, so a person whose ordinary days wobble a lot needs a large shift to cross the line. An alarm that judges each person against their own ordinary variability, measured on their days 0 to 29, works on the person's own scale. It flags 31.7% of the breaks in the least regular fifth instead of 4.3%, and 39.8% of all breaks instead of 35.8%, while it fires a little less often on ordinary days: for 4.0% of people on days 45 to 59, against 4.8% (Figure 3).

The fairness criterion called equal opportunity ([Hardt, Price and Srebro, 2016](https://arxiv.org/abs/1610.02413)) asks that the people who need to be found have the same chance of being found, whatever group they belong to. Here those are the people whose routine broke, and the groups are the fifths of regularity. With one scale for everyone, the best-seen fifth has its breaks found 55 percentage points more often than the worst-seen fifth. On each person's own scale the gap is 23 points. The most regular fifth pays a little for it: 52.0% of its breaks are flagged instead of 59.5%.

### The blind spot belongs to the detector

What remains invisible could be missing from the data, or missing from what the alarm looks at. A check answers this. Chronos-2, a forecasting model pretrained on other time series and used on the NaiveBayes tab, asks only whether a person's days stopped following that person's own past. It does not ask whether they changed the way most people changed. Within each fifth, its forecast error tells broken routines from held ones with an AUC of 0.82 in the least regular fifth and between 0.75 and 0.77 in the others (Figure 4). The habit evidence that the alarm adds up does this only weakly in the least regular fifth (AUC 0.61) and well in the most regular (0.84). So irregular people's breaks are in the data. They were different from the change most people made, and that common change is what the alarm's evidence measures.

Habit evidence on one scale, grey, and on the own scale, blue, rise together from about 61 in the least regular fifth to 59, 62, 71 and 84 in the most regular fifth. Chronos-2 forecast error, green, is highest in the least regular fifth at about 82 and stays between about 75 and 77 in the other fifths.">
The AUC for telling broken from held routines within each fifth of regularity: the habit evidence on one scale and on each person's own scale, and Chronos-2's forecast error. The habit evidence is weak for the least regular people, while Chronos-2 sees their breaks best.
</Figure>

### Whom to check first

Suppose a city can afford to check on one person in five during an emergency, for example by asking them or by updating their simulated stand-in with fresh data. Whom should it pick? Ranking people by their risk from ordinary days alone, as the pretrained TabPFN-3.5 model of the [DecTrees](../dectrees/) tab estimates it, finds 35.1% of all broken routines but only 4.6% of those in the two least regular fifths, because ordinary-day risk is highest for regular people. Ranking by the evidence of the first emergency days does better. By day 62, the evidence on each person's own scale finds 43.3% of all breaks and 31.2% of the breaks in the two least regular fifths, against 18.8% for the same evidence on one scale (Figure 5, left).

A city could also keep part of the budget for the least regular two fifths. Up to 30% of the budget, that costs nothing at all on day 62. Beyond it, it trades breaks found overall for breaks found among them: with 60% kept for them, 43.2% of their breaks are found and the total falls from 43.3% to 36.6% (right). [Bertsimas, Farias and Trichakis (2011)](https://doi.org/10.1287/opre.1100.0865) call such a trade the price of fairness. Here a moderate reserve comes free.

### What it means

Five rules follow, in plain words.

1. Irregular lives break less often, but when they break, the break is as large and much quieter.
2. An alarm built for everyone sees what its scale makes legible, which is the change made by regular people.
3. Judging each person against their own ordinary days more than halves the gap in who gets seen, with no more false alarms.
4. What stays invisible is the blind spot of the detector, not of the data: a forecaster of each person's own past sees those breaks.
5. Checking people by early evidence finds more breaks than checking them by their risk in ordinary times, and a moderate reserve for the least legible costs nothing.

For the study this matters directly. Study 4 will let an agent learn when to update itself, and an update rule learned on the average person would inherit the alarm's blind spot unless it judges each person on their own scale. For simulations of a city in a crisis, it means that the people whose simulated stand-ins fail without anyone noticing are the people whose lives were least like the average to begin with.

## Language-model stand-ins

Researchers increasingly use large language models as stand-ins for real people: a model is told about a person and asked how that person would act. The [Introduction](../introduction/) discusses how faithful such stand-ins can be. Could one have known whose routine would break? Each of the 7,135 evaluation people was turned into a short persona written only from their ordinary weeks, days 0 to 44: how often they were at home, when they were out on workdays, how regularly they went to their daytime place and how far it was, how far they ranged, and whether they went out more at weekends. Nothing from the emergency was included, and neither the person's kind of mover nor any place that could be located. Here is what one model read for one person:

<blockquote class="prompt">
<p>Here is how you have lived over the last six weeks, from the location records of your phone:</p>
<ul>
<li>About 19% of the times your location was recorded, you were at home.</li>
<li>On workdays you were away from home 98% of the time between 7 and 9 am, 97% between 10 am and 4 pm, and 87% between 6 and 10 pm.</li>
<li>On 17% of workdays you spent the middle of the day at your usual daytime place, 19.2 km from home.</li>
<li>You usually moved within about 23.2 km and passed through about 6 different 500-metre squares a day.</li>
<li>On weekends you were away from home less often than on workdays.</li>
</ul>
<p>Tomorrow an emergency begins in your city and will last about two weeks. You do not know yet what kind of emergency it is. Will your daily routine, meaning where you are at each time of the day, change noticeably compared with the last six weeks?</p>
<p>Answer with Yes or No only.</p>
</blockquote>

Two models answered as the person. [Qwen3-8B](https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen3-8B) is a general-purpose language model. [Minitaur-8B](https://huggingface.co/marcelbinz/Llama-3.1-Minitaur-8B) is the small sibling of Centaur, a model fine-tuned on about ten million choices that people made in psychology experiments in order to predict how people decide ([Binz et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-025-09215-4)). Neither was allowed to write an answer. The probability of "yes" was read directly from the probabilities each model gave to its next word. For the person above, whose routine did break, Qwen3-8B said yes with probability 95% and Minitaur-8B with 68%. For a person in the [sample](https://github.com/AntoniCzolgowski/agent-reliability-under-disruption/blob/module-3/data/samples/llm_personas_sample.csv) whose routine held, they said 97% and 65%.

Neither model could tell whose routine would break (Figure 6). Qwen3-8B ranks a person who broke above a person who held 53.5% of the time (AUC 0.535), and Minitaur-8B 49.9%, a coin flip. A single number from the person's own data, their regularity, does better (0.601), and the pretrained tabular model of the DecTrees tab better still (0.665). The reason shows in what the stand-ins expect. Qwen3-8B says yes for almost everybody, 95% on average, and Minitaur-8B 67%, while in reality 19% of routines broke. And their answers barely move with regularity: Qwen3-8B's average stays between 95% and 96% in every fifth and Minitaur-8B's at 67%, while the share that really broke climbs from 13% to 30%.

Right: by fifth of regularity, bars for the share that really broke, 13, 16, 16, 20 and 30 percent, under two flat lines for the stand-ins' average probability of yes, Qwen3-8B near 95 to 96 percent and Minitaur-8B near 67 percent.">

Built from a person's ordinary weeks, these stand-ins describe a crisis in general, in which everyone changes, rather than the person in front of them. Their answers sound plausible and carry almost no information about who will change. This is the gap between believable and faithful behaviour that the Introduction describes, measured on real people. The test has clear limits: one prompt, and two models of 8 billion parameters used without examples or fine-tuning. Larger models, other prompts or richer personas may do better, and that is worth testing next.

## Reading anonymous places through named ones

YJMob100K counts 85 categories of places in every cell but does not say what the categories are, so a cell full of category 59 could be offices, homes or bars. Study 1 asks whether the kind of place can be read from when people are there. The first step was done this month.

- **The rhythm of a cell.** For every cell with at least 500 records on days 0-59, the share of its records in each half hour, on workdays and on rest days separately. The size of the crowd is divided out, so only the timing remains.
- **Five rhythm types.** K-means sorted the 11,858 cells into five types, named by the hour at which they are busiest on a workday: 8 am, noon, 5 pm, 6 pm and 8 pm (the Clustering tab shows them). The cells form a gradient rather than separate groups, so the types describe the range of rhythms more than they find natural kinds.
- **A named mirror.** Foursquare check-ins from Tokyo carry the name of the place, folded into eight groups such as Transit, Food or Work and Institutions. The same rhythm was built for each group.
- **The comparison.** Each side minus its own average rhythm, then the cosine similarity between every YJMob type and every Foursquare group. Without subtracting the average every pair looks alike (0.42 to 0.96), because all places share the basic shape of a day.

<Figure n={7} title="YJMob place rhythms against Foursquare place groups" src={f31} alt="Heat map with five rows for the YJMob rhythm types, peak 08:00, 12:00, 17:00, 18:00 and 20:00, and eight columns for the Foursquare groups from Transit to Home, coloured from blue for minus 1 to red for plus 1. The strongest match in each row is in bold: Work and Institutions 0.71 for peak 08:00, Work and Institutions 0.83 for peak 12:00, Shopping and Services 0.54 for peak 17:00, Nightlife 0.88 for peak 18:00, Home 0.76 for peak 20:00.">
How closely the daily rhythm of each YJMob type of cell follows the rhythm of each Foursquare place group, after removing each source's average day, from -1 (opposite) to 1 (same shape). The closest group in each row is in bold.
</Figure>

What came out is a consistent picture. The noon cells and the 8 am cells look like Work and Institutions (0.83 and 0.71), the 5 pm cells like Shopping and Services (0.54), the 6 pm cells like Nightlife (0.88) and the 8 pm cells like Home (0.76). The busiest-at-noon type is also the one that lost the most records in the emergency, 15% on workdays, which fits a workplace that fewer people came to.

What it does not show is just as important. A rhythm says when people are in a cell, not what the cell contains, and the two sources record people differently: YJMob records a phone, Foursquare a person who chose to check in, which is why evening leisure and home look so alike here. The check-ins come from another provider and from 2012-13, and the two grids are never aligned on a map. The labels above are inferred and reported as such; they are not names the dataset contains, and no attempt is made to identify the city.

Next comes the harder step of the study: estimating a rhythm for each of the 85 categories separately, by treating a cell's rhythm as a mix of the rhythms of the places it holds, and testing the method first on Foursquare with the names hidden, where the right answer is known.

## Signals for an agent that updates itself

Study 4 asks whether an agent can learn when to update its picture of a person. That needs a signal it could watch. The PCA tab measured two on real data: how far a person's fortnight sits from the centre of their own kind of mover, and how much of it the five frozen components cannot reconstruct. Both grow in the emergency (median 2.81 to 3.15 and 1.65 to 1.80) and grow most for regular commuters. They will be among the candidate inputs when the update agent is built in November.

## The question

The course tabs build the standard ML models on this data. The research asks a additional question : when does an agent built from a person's ordinary days stop being a reliable description of that person, and how much fresh evidence brings it back? YJMob100K makes this testable, because it follows the same 25,000 people through 60 ordinary days and 15 days of an emergency.

## Four studies

*Study 1, place semantics (supporting).* Foursquare check-ins carry real place types, so I can test how well the kind of place is predicted from context alone: the cell, the hour, the weekday and the person's history. The classifier tabs of the course do this work. A second, exploratory step asks whether the 85 anonymous place categories of YJMob100K can be recovered from the daily rhythm of each cell, validated first on Foursquare with the names hidden. Any recovered label will be reported as inferred, never as a feature of the dataset.

*Study 2, behaviour change (supporting).* Person profiles from the ordinary days, clustering and PCA on the course tabs, and a plain description of what changed between days 45 to 59 and 60 to 74. The observation rate is reported as an outcome in its own right, since being recorded at all changes with the emergency.

*Study 3, reliability (core).* Simple agents of each person's movement, from a last-cell rule to a learned mix of long-run and recent habits, trained on days 0 to 44, validated on 45 to 59 and frozen. Each agent is tested on days 67 to 74 with 0, 1, 3 or 7 days of post-change data, under equal information, so the update mechanism is a thing that differs. Context contains: movement history alone, history plus the anonymous place counts, and history plus the Study 1 semantics if its validation passes. Outcomes are next-position error, short rollouts of 3 and 7 steps, and improvement over the frozen agent, all by pre-change behaviour group. Two hypotheses: ordinary-period validation does not fully predict post-change reliability, and fresh evidence helps with diminishing returns. Both can fail.

*Study 4, a learned update schedule (RL extension).* Study 3 fixes when an agent is updated. Study 4 lets an agent learn it. Under a budget of k updates, an agent sees a small state each day, yesterday's error, its trend, updates left and the weekly phase, and chooses to keep or to update. The reward is the negative prediction error, scored by replaying the recorded observations. Tabular Q-learning written from scratch in NumPy, trained on pre-emergency days only, frozen before day 60, and compared under the same budget with never updating, updating on the first k days, the most recent k days, and a threshold rule. If the threshold rule is enough, that is the finding.

## Limits

One emergency in one undisclosed city, so nothing transfers to emergencies in general. The study does not try to identify the city or any person, and it does not align the grid with a map. Results that go against the hypotheses will be reported like any others.

The findings of October 2026 add limits of their own. Whether a routine broke is measured against one simple habit model, so for people it described poorly the label is noisier; the share that broke is the same for people who are recorded rarely and often, which suggests that recording is not what drives it. The alarms were set on ordinary days and tested on the emergency of the same evaluation people, so their numbers come from one emergency and one group of people. The language-model test used one prompt and two small models without examples. The code, seeds and logs behind every number are linked from the course tabs.
