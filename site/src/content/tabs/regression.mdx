<p class="lede">When an emergency starts, a simulated person built from ordinary weeks has to decide what each new day means: a one-off, or the first day of a new routine? In ordinary weeks an unusual day is usually a one-off, and an agent that took it seriously would get worse. Linear regression measures how much a new day should count, and the answer changes with the situation: in the emergency one new day deserves four times more weight than in ordinary weeks, as much as a whole ordinary week of new days. Weighted that way, a week of fresh days brings the agent back to the accuracy it had before the emergency began. Logistic regression then returns to the question of the NaiveBayes tab, whether a single day comes from the emergency, and answers it better than any Naive Bayes model did.</p>

## Overview

**Linear regression** predicts a number. It assumes the number is a weighted sum of the inputs plus a constant: prediction = b₀ + b₁ × input₁ + b₂ × input₂ + … The weights are found by **least squares**: among all possible lines (or planes, with more inputs), it takes the one with the smallest sum of squared errors between the predictions and the real values on the training examples (Figure 1, left). Each weight has a plain meaning. It is the change in the prediction when its input grows by one unit and the other inputs stay the same, the slope of the line (Figure 1, right). Linear regression is used wherever a number has to be predicted and the reasons have to be read: a price from the size of a flat, demand for electricity from the temperature, or here, how a person spends next week from how they spent the weeks before.

<Figure n={1} title="Linear regression: a line, its errors and a slope" src={o11} alt="Two panels with toy data. Left: twelve blue points, the share of a usual fortnight away from home on the horizontal axis and the share of the next week on the vertical axis, with a black fitted line 0.01 plus 0.96 times the habit and short orange vertical segments from each point to the line marking the errors. Right: the same line with a green step: 10 more points of habit give 9.6 more points predicted for the next week.">
Left: least squares picks the line that makes the squared vertical errors, the orange segments, as small as possible. Right: a weight is the slope of the line, here 9.6 more points of predicted share for every 10 more points of habit.
</Figure>

**Logistic regression** predicts a class, here whether a day comes from the emergency or from ordinary weeks. It starts with the same kind of weighted sum, called a score, but a score can be any number, while a probability has to lie between 0 and 1. The **sigmoid** function, P = 1 / (1 + e^−score), squeezes any score into that range: a score of 0 gives 0.5, large positive scores approach 1 and large negative scores approach 0 (Figure 2). The probability becomes a class by a cut, usually at 0.5. The weights are found by maximising the likelihood of the training labels, not by least squares. Each weight now has a meaning in **odds**, the probability of the class divided by the probability of the other class: raising an input by one unit multiplies the odds by e^weight. A weight of 0.11 multiplies the odds by 1.12, so they become 12% higher.

<Figure n={2} title="Logistic regression: from a score to a probability to a class" src={o12} alt="Three panels. Left: horizontal bars for the contributions of five toy inputs to a score, a starting value of minus 0.3, seen elsewhere at 12:00 plus 0.11, at home at 20:00 plus 0.04, at the place at 09:00 minus 0.05 and 17 seen half hours plus 0.62, adding up to a score of 0.42. Middle: the S-shaped sigmoid curve from 0 to 1, with the score 0.42 mapped to a probability of 0.60 and a dashed line at 0.5. Right: text saying that 0.60 is above 0.5, so the day is predicted as an emergency day, and that a weight of 0.11 multiplies the odds by 1.12.">
A score adds up weighted inputs (left), the sigmoid turns it into a probability (middle), and a cut at 0.5 turns the probability into a class (right). The numbers are made up to show the steps.
</Figure>

The two differ in what they predict and in what their weights mean. Linear regression predicts a number on its own scale and its weights are slopes in that scale. Logistic regression predicts a probability of a class and its weights are changes in the log of the odds. Both are linear in their inputs, which is why both are easy to read and why neither can bend around a pattern on its own.

**Logistic regression and Naive Bayes are a known pair.** Naive Bayes learns how each class produces the data and assumes that every half hour of a day is independent of the others once the class is known. Logistic regression learns the boundary between the classes directly and makes no such assumption, so when two half hours carry the same information, it does not count it twice. With enough data, logistic regression is expected to do at least as well. This tab tests that on the same days and people as the NaiveBayes tab.

In this project, a simulated person, an **agent**, is built from a person's ordinary weeks and stands in for them in a simulation of the city. The NaiveBayes and DecTrees tabs found that the emergency broke about one routine in five, and that the warning in ordinary weeks was faint. This tab asks what an agent should do once the emergency has started and the first new days come in. **That is a question only regression can answer, because the answer is a weight: how much of a new day to believe, and how much of the old habit to keep.**

## Data

### Linear regression: one row per person

The number to predict is the **share of the day spent away from home**: of the half hours in which a person is seen, the share in which they are not in their home block (the 3 × 3 squares around where they spend their nights, as on the NaiveBayes tab). It is measured over the **second week of the emergency, days 67 to 74**. The inputs are what an agent could know on that week's eve:

- **The habit**: the same share over the 45 days before the emergency (days 15 to 59), kept separately for workdays and rest days and mixed in the proportion of workdays and rest days of the week to predict.
- **The first days**: how far the share of the first k emergency days, starting on day 60, sat from what the habit expected on those days, for k = 1, 2, 3, 5 and 7. A first day spent more away from home than usual gives a positive number.
- **Traits** from the DecTrees tab, all from days 0 to 44: regularity, the commute in km, how far a person ranges, records per day, and the kind of mover as six 0/1 columns.

The same table is built a second time for **ordinary weeks**, as a placebo with exactly the same shape: the habit from days 0 to 44, the first days from day 45 and the week to predict 52 to 59. Comparing the two arms shows what is special about the emergency, because anything that also happens between two ordinary weeks is not.

A day counts if a person is seen in at least 4 of its half hours (00:00 left out, as before). 23,476 of the 23,904 labelled people of Part 3 have a readable habit and at least 3 readable days in both weeks to predict. Table 1 shows six of them.

### Logistic regression: one row per person-day

Logistic regression needs a two-class label, and it uses the label of the NaiveBayes tab: is a person-day from the ordinary fortnight (days 45 to 59, class 0) or from the emergency (days 60 to 74, class 1)? The days are the same 677,295 readable person-days, written as 47 half hours, each unseen, at home, at the daytime place or elsewhere (Table 3). The people are split exactly as before: 379,586 days of 13,732 people for training, 94,965 days of 3,432 other development people for validation, and 202,744 days of 7,350 evaluation people for the test (Table 4). Two sets of inputs are compared:

- **The clock**: the same 282 inputs as the multinomial Naive Bayes model, one 0/1 column for each combination of day type, half hour and place, such as "workday, 12:00, elsewhere".
- **The person**: 47 numbers, one per half hour, saying how unusual the person's state in that half hour was for them, measured as the surprise of their own habit model from days 0 to 44 (0 when the half hour is not seen).

## Results

### Before the regression: the emergency reshuffled people

On average, the emergency hardly moved the share of the day people spent away from home. The habit says 67.9% and the second emergency week shows 69.0%, a change of 1.1 points, against 1.4 points between two ordinary weeks. **The emergency did not shift everyone the same way. It moved people in different directions.** Regular commuters spent 1.6 points less of the week away from home than their habit, while homebodies spent 4.0 points more and local regulars 4.4 points more. An agent that knows only the average change would learn nothing, because there is almost no average change to learn.

### The linear model

Four sets of inputs were compared by 5-fold cross-validation on the training people, with a light Ridge penalty (a small cost on large weights) tried at five strengths. The error is the root mean squared error, **RMSE**, in percentage points of the share away from home: roughly, how far a typical prediction misses. A point is one percent of a person's day. On a 16-hour waking day that is about 10 minutes, so an error of 16 points means missing how long someone is away from home by about two and a half hours a day, and 10.6 points by about an hour and 40 minutes.

It was then fitted on all training people and scored once on the test people:

**R²** is the share of the differences between people that the model explains, and **MAE** is the mean absolute error. In ordinary weeks, a person's habit alone explains 85% of how much of the next week they spend away from home. In the emergency the same habit explains 59%, and its error almost doubles. That drop is this project's question, when an agent stops being a reliable description of its person, measured as one number. With the first three emergency days the model explains 82% and the error falls to 10.6 points. Figure 3 shows the predictions against what people really did.

Two weights in plain words, from the chosen model:

- **Habit, 0.90.** Take two people whose first emergency days went as their habits predicted. If one of them usually spends 10% more of the day away from home, about an hour and a half more of a 16-hour waking day, the model expects them to spend 9% more of the second emergency week away, about an hour and 25 minutes more per day. The old routine still sets most of the level.
- **The first three days, 0.68.** If someone's first three emergency days kept them away from home for 10% more of the day than their habit expected, about an hour and a half extra, the model expects them to spend 6.8% more of the second week away, a little over an hour more per day. Two thirds of an early change carry into the following week.

Two traits also matter a little: weekend movers spend 5.3% less of the week away than their habit and first days suggest, about 50 minutes a day, and homebodies 3.7% less, about 35 minutes.

### A one-off or a new routine: how much a new day should count

To see what an agent should do day by day, the model was refitted with only two inputs, the habit and the first k days, for each k and in both arms, with 95% intervals from 500 bootstrap samples of training people (Figure 4, right). In ordinary weeks, the first new day gets a weight of 0.11 (0.10 to 0.13). In the emergency it gets 0.46 (0.44 to 0.47). **In ordinary weeks an unusual day is usually a one-off. In the emergency it is usually the start of a new routine, and four times as much of it is worth believing: one emergency day counts as much as seven ordinary days (0.46 against 0.47).**

The same weights can be read as a mix of the old and the new. After one new day, the best prediction in ordinary weeks is 0.86 × habit + 0.11 × the new day. In the emergency it is 0.48 × habit + 0.46 × the new day, an equal mix. After seven days in the emergency it is 0.17 × habit + 0.83 × the new days: the old routine has almost stopped counting.

The left of Figure 4 is a **repair curve**. It compares four agents on the test people, after 0 to 7 fresh emergency days:

- The **frozen habit** never looks at the new days. Its error stays at 16.0 points for the whole emergency.
- **Fresh days only** forgets the habit and believes the new days alone. After one day it is worse than the habit (16.5 points), because one day is a noisy sample of a week. It catches up as days accumulate (8.9 points after seven).
- **The mix tuned in ordinary weeks** carries over the weights of normal times. It improves slowly, to 12.8 points after three days and 10.6 after seven, because it keeps treating the first days of a new routine as one-offs.
- **The mix fitted in the emergency** uses the weights the emergency calls for. It is at 12.8 points after one day, 10.8 after three and 8.9 after seven, which is back at the 8.8 points of an ordinary week.

So a week of the new situation, weighed correctly, gives an agent back its ordinary accuracy. Weighed with the habits of normal times, the same week gets it only halfway.

### Whose agents need the most repair

Figure 5 splits the errors by kind of mover. The emergency roughly doubled the error for every kind, and three fresh days closed between 59% and 81% of the gap to the ordinary week. Far roamers keep the smallest errors throughout. Weekend movers, local regulars and homebodies start with the largest errors even in ordinary weeks and remain the hardest to describe. For the people whose routine broke, by the label of the DecTrees tab, the habit's error nearly triples, from 8.8 to 24.0 points, and three days bring it down to 13.3. For people whose routine held, the emergency still adds 4.5 points of error, so it touched more lives than the label counts.

### Logistic regression against Naive Bayes

The strength of the penalty on large weights, C (smaller C means a stronger penalty), was chosen on the validation people by log loss, the same way the smoothing of Naive Bayes was chosen (Figure 6, left and middle). With 380,000 training days the choice barely matters: every C from 0.0001 upward gives nearly the same validation score, and only the strongest penalties make it worse. The final models were fitted on all development people and scored once on the test days.

On exactly the same 282 inputs, **logistic regression beats multinomial Naive Bayes clearly**: accuracy 58.2% against 52.7%, AUC 0.603 against 0.538. It also beats the best Naive Bayes model, the Bernoulli one. And unlike every Naive Bayes model, its log loss is below that of a coin flip (0.693): its probabilities are modest and honest, where the Naive Bayes probabilities were overconfident. The reason is the independence assumption. Neighbouring half hours of a day repeat each other, so Naive Bayes counts the same evidence many times and pushes its probabilities too far, while logistic regression learns how much each half hour adds beyond the others.

How unusual each half hour was for the person carries little on its own (AUC 0.528), but added to the clock it lifts AUC to 0.621, the best day-level score in the project so far. Majority guessing would score 51.0%.

### What the weights say

Each weight of the clock model is an odds ratio for one half hour and one place. Figure 8 (left) compares being at home, or at the daytime place, with being elsewhere, half hour by half hour on workdays. One weight in plain words: **on a workday, being seen at one's daytime place at 12:00 rather than elsewhere multiplies the odds that the day is an emergency day by 0.90.** Put the other way round, being somewhere else at noon raises the odds by 11%. Being at home in the evening, from 19:00 to 21:30, raises them by 3% to 7% per half hour, while being at home in the morning rush, 07:00 to 08:30, lowers them by about 7%.

No single half hour comes close to giving the emergency away. Between 06:00 and 22:00 every odds ratio lies between 0.90 and 1.07, and the model is right on 58% of days only by adding dozens of such nudges together. The larger values at night, up to 1.19 for the daytime place at 03:00, rest on the few people seen at their daytime place at that hour. This matches the NaiveBayes tab: on any one day, the emergency is quiet. The person model (Figure 8, right) says when being unusual for oneself matters. Being unusual in the small hours, 02:00 to 05:00, points to an emergency day, while being unusual late in the evening points slightly to an ordinary one.

## Conclusions

The emergency did not move the average person: people spent almost the same share of their days away from home as before. It moved people in different directions, and that is what made their simulated stand-ins fail. A person's ordinary weeks explain 85% of how they spend the next ordinary week, but only 59% of how they spend a week of the emergency.

The main finding is about how an agent should learn once something has changed. In ordinary weeks an unusual day is usually a one-off: the best weight for one new day is 0.11, and an agent that believed new days fully would get worse. In the emergency an unusual day is usually the start of a new routine: the best weight is 0.46, four times larger and as large as the weight of a whole ordinary week, and after a week the old habit is worth only 0.17. An agent weighted for the emergency gets back to its ordinary accuracy after about seven days. One that keeps the weights of normal times gets only halfway in the same week. **The right amount of trust in new evidence is not a fixed setting. It depends on the situation, so an agent first has to recognise that the situation has changed.**

That is where logistic regression comes in. Read half hour by half hour, the clock of a single day tells an emergency day from an ordinary one better than any Naive Bayes model (AUC 0.603 against at most 0.591), and adding how unusual each half hour was for the person raises it to 0.621. Still, a single day is a faint signal, made of many nudges of a few percent each. Recognising the emergency takes several days or many people at once, not one person's one day.

For anyone building simulations of a city, this adds two working rules to those of the earlier tabs.

**Let an agent's trust in new data depend on the situation.** A weight tuned in normal weeks reads the first days of a disruption as one-offs. In this data it left the agent missing people's time away from home by about an hour and 40 minutes a day after a week, where the right weight brought it back to an hour and 25 minutes, the error of an ordinary week. Give every agent two settings, one for normal times and one for a disruption, and let a detector like the alarm of the NaiveBayes tab switch between them.

**Get fresh data in the first days of a disruption and update the agents every day.** Those days are worth more than any other data a simulation will get: one emergency day counts as much as a whole ordinary week. When a disruption begins, the first job is to collect what people do now, from phone records, transit counts or a short check-in, and update the agents daily through the first week instead of waiting for things to settle. After about a week they are back to their normal accuracy.

These results are also the first measured payoff for the update agent of the [Research](../research/) tab: an update after the first emergency days is worth far more than an update in ordinary weeks. Learning when to update starts from that difference.
