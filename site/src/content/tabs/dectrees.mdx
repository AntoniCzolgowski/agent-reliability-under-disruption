<p class="lede">In the emergency, about one person in five had to reorganise their daily life, and the routine of their ordinary weeks broke. This tab asks which habits, visible long before, made a person more likely to be one of them. Signs like these would tell a city whom to check on first when an emergency begins, and tell a planner which simulated people to trust least. Decision trees turn ordinary weeks into a few readable questions, and the clearest sign they find is a surprise: the more regular a person's weeks, the more likely their routine was to break.</p>

## Overview

A decision tree predicts by asking a short series of yes-or-no questions, each one chosen according to the answer to the one before. Is this person's regularity above 0.7? If yes, do they travel more than 600 m to their daytime place? Each question splits the examples into two groups, and the questions are drawn like an upside-down tree. The first question at the top is the root, every question is a node, and every group at the end of a path is a leaf that holds the prediction, usually the class that most of its training examples belong to. Trees are used where a decision has to be explained: deciding who gets a loan, sorting patients in an emergency room, finding the fault in a machine or choosing which customers to call first. A person can read a small tree and check every step, and that is its main strength.

Every question looks at one feature and cuts it at one value. So a tree divides the space of all features into boxes, and every box gets one answer (Figure 1). Two questions make three boxes in the example; deeper trees make smaller and more numerous boxes.

A tree is **trained** from the top down. At the root, the algorithm tries every feature and every possible cut and keeps the question that best separates the classes. Then it repeats the same search inside each of the two new groups, and so on, until a group is pure, too small to split or a chosen depth is reached. The search is greedy: each question is the best one at that moment, without looking ahead to later questions. To **predict**, a new example starts at the root, follows its own answers down to a leaf and receives that leaf's class. **The share of training examples of each class in the leaf can also be read as a probability.**

"Best separates" needs a measure of how mixed a group is, called **impurity**. Let p be the share of a group that belongs to one class, here the share whose routine broke. **Gini impurity** is 1 − p² − (1 − p)²: the chance that two examples drawn at random from the group belong to different classes. It is 0 for a pure group and 0.5 for a group split 50:50. **Entropy** is −p log₂ p − (1 − p) log₂ (1 − p), measured in bits: how much information is still missing to know an example's class. It is 0 for a pure group and 1 for a 50:50 group (Figure 2, left). A split is judged by how much it lowers impurity. The impurity after a split is the average of the two new groups' impurities, each weighted by its share of the examples, and the drop from before to after is called the **information gain** when entropy is used. The tree takes the question with the largest gain. Gini and entropy nearly always pick the same questions, and Gini is slightly faster to compute.

Here is one split worked by hand on this tab's training people (Figure 2, right): is a person's regularity, the share of workdays they spent at their usual daytime place, above 0.71?

For all training people the share who broke is p = 3,226 / 16,769 = 0.19238, so entropy is −0.19238 × log₂ 0.19238 − 0.80762 × log₂ 0.80762 = 0.70642 bits, and Gini is 1 − 0.19238² − 0.80762² = 0.31074. The two groups after the split are worked out the same way. Weighted by their sizes, their entropy is (11,896 × 0.62593 + 4,873 × 0.85533) / 16,769 = 0.69259 bits. The information gain is 0.70642 − 0.69259 = 0.01383 bits, and Gini drops by 0.31074 − 0.30446 = 0.00628. The gain is small: knowing whether someone's regularity is above 0.71 says only a little about whether their routine will break. The group above 0.71 is even more mixed than the starting group, because 28% is closer to 50:50 than 19% is. The gain comes from the much larger group below 0.71 becoming a little purer. When the tree searched every possible cut by itself, it chose 0.725, close to this hand-picked value.

It is generally possible to build an **endless number of different trees** from the same data. A cut can sit anywhere between two neighbouring values, so infinitely many cuts, such as 0.71 and 0.725 here, sort the people almost the same way. A tree can stop at any depth and require any minimum number of examples in a leaf. Features that carry similar information can stand in for each other. And a slightly different sample of the same people produces a different tree, because the greedy search follows small differences. The Results show 200 trees, each grown on a resampled copy of the training people, and 139 of them are different.

A tree that keeps splitting until every leaf is pure fits its training examples perfectly, noise included, and then predicts new examples badly. This is called **overfitting**. The usual cures are to stop growing early, with a maximum depth or a minimum number of examples per leaf chosen by cross-validation, or to grow the tree fully and then **prune** it, removing the branches that help least. Both appear in the Results.

In this project, the [NaiveBayes](../naivebayes/) tab found, after the fact, whose routine broke in the emergency: each person has a **habit model**, a description of where they usually are at each half hour, learned from their ordinary days, and for 19.1% of people that model became far more surprised by their days in the emergency than chance allows. A broken routine means that a person had to reorganise their days: they were no longer where their habits put them at the usual times. That is not harm in itself, but it marks the people whose lives the emergency reached, and whom services, transport and information then have to find in new places. Here decision trees ask whether this could have been foreseen, and from which behaviours. Each person is described only by numbers from days 0 to 44, long before anything happened, and the label is whether their routine broke on days 60 to 74.

A tree's answer is a short list of behaviours that anyone can check, which is what a decision maker needs: a city could use it to decide whom to look after first, before anything has gone wrong for them. It matters for simulations as well. A **simulated stand-in** is a software agent built from a person's records to act as that person in a simulation of the city. **If ordinary weeks carry a warning, then a planner, or a simulation built from those weeks, could know in advance which people's simulated stand-ins to trust least once an emergency starts.** The [warning signs](#warning-signs) at the end of the Results gather what the trees found in plain words.

## Data

Decision trees are supervised, so they need labelled data: one row per person, with numbers that describe them and the class they belong to. The class, "broke" or "held", comes from the NaiveBayes tab. It is available for the 23,904 people with enough records in every fortnight, of whom 19.1% broke.

Every feature comes from days 0 to 44 only, so nothing from the emergency can leak into the prediction. There are 34 of them:

- **The fifteen traits of the Clustering tab**, averaged over days 0 to 44: how far a person ranges (for example how many kilometres their records typically lie from the middle of all their positions), how rooted they are (for example the share of records at home) and when they are out (for example the share of workday middays spent away from home). One of them is **regularity**, the share of workdays on which the person spends the middle of the day at their usual daytime place. Put simply, it measures how alike a person's workdays are: a regularity of 0.9 means being at the same place around midday on nine workdays out of ten, like someone with a fixed office, shop or classroom, and a regularity near 0 means spending almost every midday somewhere different, like a courier or a salesperson on the road.
- **Three recording traits**: records per day, the share of days with any record, and the hours between the first and last record of a day. They describe how well a person is recorded rather than how they live.
- **Habit surprise on days 30 to 44**: how surprised a habit model learned on days 0 to 29 is by the person's days 30 to 44, a measure of how predictable the person was in ordinary times.
- **Commute**: the distance in kilometres between home and the daytime place, measured between the centres of their 500 m squares. It is 0 for about a quarter of people, whose usual daytime place is in their home square.
- **Places at home and at the daytime place**: the logarithm of the number of places of any kind that YJMob100K counts in the home square and in the daytime square.
- **The kind of mover** from the Clustering tab and **the daily rhythm of the daytime place**, the type of the square where the person spends workday middays, also from the Clustering tab (busiest at 8 am, noon, 5 pm, 6 pm or 8 pm, or a quiet square with too few records to give it a type).

Scikit-learn's trees need numbers only, so the kind of mover and the rhythm of the daytime place, which are categories, are turned into 0/1 columns, one per category: a person who is a far roamer has a 1 in the "far roamer" column and 0 in the five other kind columns (Table 2). No values are missing, so nothing had to be filled in.

The training and test sets are split **by person**, the same way as on every tab. On the DataPrep_EDA tab every person was assigned once, at random, to a development group or an evaluation group. The 16,769 labelled development people train the trees, and the 7,135 labelled evaluation people are the test set (Table 3). The two sets share nobody. A disjoint test set is the only honest way to see how a tree does on people it has never seen. If test people were also in training, a deep tree could simply memorise them, and the test score would say nothing about anyone new. Both sets hold almost the same share of people who broke, 19.2% and 18.9%. Table 4 shows six test people.

Only about one person in five broke. A tree that always answered "held" would be right for 81.1% of the test people while finding nobody who broke. So the trees are trained with **balanced class weights**, which make every person who broke count about four times as much as a person who held, and the results report balanced accuracy and AUC next to plain accuracy.

## Results

### Three trees

Three trees were grown on the training people and tested on the evaluation people. **Tree 1** is cut at depth 2, so that it is a rule anyone can say out loud. **Tree 2** is tuned by 5-fold cross-validation: the training people are split into five parts, each setting is trained on four parts and scored on the fifth, five times over, and the setting with the best average AUC is kept. The search covered Gini and entropy, maximum depths from 2 to 10 and minimum leaf sizes from 10 to 200 people. **Tree 3** is grown fully, with no limits at all.

Accuracy alone is misleading here, because always answering "held" scores 81.1%. **Balanced accuracy** is the average of two shares, the share of people who broke that are found and the share of people who held that are left alone, so guessing scores 50% and a perfect tree 100%. **AUC** asks how often a tree ranks a randomly chosen person who broke above a randomly chosen person who held. Figure 3 shows all three trees on the 7,135 test people.

Tree 2, tuned, accuracy 58.3 percent, balanced 59.4 percent: 3,337, 2,452, 523 and 823. Tree 3, grown fully, accuracy 70.8 percent, balanced 52.6 percent: 4,735, 1,054, 1,030 and 316.">
The three trees on the 7,135 test people, with the count and the row share in each cell. Tree 2 finds the most people who broke (61%) at the price of flagging 42% of those who held, and Tree 3 does little better than guessing.

The confusion matrices also raise a question that no single score answers: which mistake matters more? A tree can be wrong in two ways. It can raise a **false alarm**, flagging a person whose routine then held, or it can **miss** a person whose routine then broke. In a system meant to look after people, the two do not cost the same. A false alarm costs a call, a visit or an offer of help that turns out not to be needed. A miss means that someone whose daily life is about to be upended is not looked for until it has already happened. Such a system should be judged first by the share of the people who broke that it finds, called its **recall**, and only then by its false alarms. The balanced class weights already lean this way, because they count each person who broke about four times as much as a person who held. In Figure 3, Tree 2 makes this trade: it finds twice as many of the people who broke as Tree 1 does, and pays for it with many more false alarms. With a warning this faint, finding most of the people at risk means checking almost half of everyone, 3,275 of the 7,135 test people. That still beats chance: Tree 2 needs about four checks for every person it finds, where checking people at random would need more than five. So the real decision is how many checks a city can afford for every person it does not want to miss.

Tree 1 is right for 73.8% of test people, but it finds only 410 of the 1,346 people who broke (30%); its balanced accuracy is 57.2% and its AUC 0.600. Its rule is short (Figure 4). The root asks about regularity and cuts it at 0.725. Both branches then ask about the commute, and only one leaf predicts "broke": people whose regularity is above 0.725 and whose commute is longer than 0.6 km. That cut sits close to home. It separates the people whose daytime place is at home or right beside it, about three people in ten, from everyone who travels to it, and in the sample of 500 people linked in the Data section, half of those who fit the rule travel more than 7.5 km. **In plain words, the tree flags people who spend most workday middays at one fixed place away from home: about one test person in five fits, and three in ten of them broke, nearly twice the rate of everyone else (16%). The logic is that such a person's whole day hangs on one trip to one place, so when an emergency disrupts the trip or the place, the day has to be rebuilt, while a person whose days happen near home or already vary has less to rebuild.**

Cross-validation chose Gini, a maximum depth of 10 and at least 200 people in every leaf, which gives Tree 2 54 leaves (Figure 5). Gini and entropy came out the same to three decimals (cross-validated AUC 0.633). The chosen depth and leaf size are the largest that were tried, so a wider search might do slightly better. On the test people Tree 2 has the lowest accuracy of the three, 58.3%, but the highest balanced accuracy, 59.4%, and the highest AUC, 0.629. It finds 823 of the 1,346 people who broke (61%) while flagging 2,452 of the 5,789 who held (42%). Its first two levels are the same questions as Tree 1. Regularity carries 32% of all its splitting work, the share of records at home 16% and the commute 14%, with everything else far behind.

Tree 3 grows to 2,779 leaves, 48 questions deep, and gets every training person right. On the test people it does little better than guessing: balanced accuracy 52.6%, AUC 0.526. It learned the training people, not a rule about people. Its 70.8% accuracy comes from answering "held" for most people, which is easy when four in five held. This failure also says something about people. To get every training person right, the tree had to cut them into 2,779 groups of about six people on average, and those fine distinctions did not carry over to anyone new: people who looked alike on these 34 measures still went different ways in the emergency. It is the same finding as on the NaiveBayes tab, reached from another side. People who look the same on the surface do not live the same lives, so they have to be followed one by one rather than sorted into predefined types and scenarios.

### Pruning, and why there is never one tree

Pruning shows the same thing step by step (Figure 6, left). Starting from the full tree, branches are removed in the order in which they add the least, and each pruned tree is scored again. Balanced accuracy on the training people falls from 100% to 61%, while on the test people it rises from 52.6% at 2,779 leaves to 58.6% at 11 leaves. Here, **smaller trees are better trees**.

Then 200 depth-2 trees were grown, each on a different resampled copy of the training people, drawn with replacement so that some people appear twice and others not at all (Figure 6, right). All 200 ask about regularity first, but they cut it anywhere between 0.61 and 0.82. Their second questions vary: 88 trees ask about the commute on both sides, 75 about the commute on one side and the share of records at home on the other, and smaller numbers pair the commute with records at night, middays away from home or the length of moves. 139 of the 200 trees are different, and yet their test balanced accuracy only ranges from 56.8% to 59.3%. Many different trees fit these people almost equally well, which is why no single tree's exact cuts should be read as the truth. What all of them share, a first question about regularity, is the part to trust.

### Who breaks

Behind every tree is one plain pattern that needs no model at all (Figure 7). Among the most regular fifth of people, 30.0% broke, twice as many as among the least regular fifth (14.1%), and the share rises with every fifth: 14.1%, 15.0%, 15.7%, 20.9% and 30.0%. By kind of mover, regular commuters broke most (28.8%) and mid-range movers least (13.8%). **People whose daytime place is busiest at noon, the rhythm that looked most like workplaces on the Clustering tab, broke more often (22.1%) than people whose daytime place is busiest in the evening or a quiet square (16.7% to 16.9%).** This sign belongs to places as much as to people. A city can tell which of its areas fill up at midday from counts of how many people are there at each hour, without following anyone, and it can reach the people who spend their working days there through the places themselves, such as their workplaces.

This is the surprise. Regularity is what makes a person easy to describe: someone who goes to the same place every workday is the easiest person to predict, and a simulated stand-in built from their ordinary weeks looks most reliable in ordinary times. **Those are the people whose routines the emergency broke most often.** A fixed workday rhythm has the most to lose when ordinary workdays stop.

### A check by pretrained models

Trees are readable but not the most accurate models. As a check, three pretrained tabular models read the same 34 features of the same people. **TabICLv2** and **TabPFN-3.5** are transformer networks pretrained on millions of synthetic tables. They are not trained on this data at all: **they read the training people as examples and predict the test people in a single pass, much as a language model answers a question from examples in its prompt.** **TabPFN-3.5 Thinking** is the strongest setting of the TabPFN family, which spends extra computing time on Prior Labs' servers searching for the best way to prepare the table, here told to maximise AUC. The table sent to the servers held only the numbers, with no person ids.

All three do better than the best tree (Figure 8). Their AUCs are 0.662, 0.665 and 0.663, against 0.629 for Tree 2, and their balanced accuracy lies between 60.6% and 62.1%. Compared on the same 2,000 resamples of the test people, the gain over Tree 2 is between 0.033 and 0.035, with 95% intervals between 0.021 and 0.047, so it is small but real. The Thinking setting adds nothing over the standard TabPFN-3.5. Even the strongest model available ranks a person who broke above a person who held only two times in three.

### Warning signs

Put together, the trees and the plain counts describe the people whose routine the emergency was most likely to break, using only behaviour that could be seen in their ordinary weeks:

- **The same place at midday, workday after workday.** Regularity is the first question of every tree, and the most regular fifth of people broke about twice as often as the least regular fifth (30.0% against 14.1%).
- **A daytime place away from home.** At least 187 of the 200 resampled trees ask about the commute next. Among the regular training people, about a third of those whose daytime place is more than about 600 m from home broke, against about one in five of those whose daytime place is at home or beside it.
- **A daytime place that fills up at noon.** Of the daily rhythms of places, this one looks most like workplaces, and 22.1% of the people who spend their workdays at such a place broke, against about 17% at places busiest in the evening and at quiet squares.

Together these signs describe the regular commuter, the kind of mover whose routine broke most often (28.8%). One pattern points the other way. People whose days already vary, the least regular fifth, the mid-range movers and the far roamers, broke least often (14% to 16%). Lower risk is not no risk, though: their breaks were as large, only much harder to see, as the [Research](../research/#invisible-breaks) tab shows.

The signs raise a person's odds; they do not decide them. Tree 1's two questions flag about one test person in five, and three in ten of them broke, but seven in ten held, and seven in ten of all the people who broke were never flagged. So the signs set an order of where to look first, such as which simulated stand-ins to refresh first and which areas and workplaces to reach first with information and help, rather than a list of the people who will need it. And because they all point at regular lives, checking people by these signs alone would find about a third of all broken routines but almost none among the least regular people. Whether a particular person's days have actually changed is a question for an alarm that watches that person against their own ordinary weeks, as on the NaiveBayes tab, and the [Research](../research/#whom-to-check-first) tab shows how evidence from the first days of an emergency finds many of the breaks that these signs miss.

## Conclusions

Ordinary weeks gave only a faint warning of whose routine would break. The best readable tree ranks a person who broke above a person who held about 63% of the time, and the strongest pretrained models manage 66%. Most of who would break was not written in their ordinary weeks, at least not in any of the 34 ways they were measured here. Where people go tells only part of how they will cope. Decision systems for real emergencies will need to ground their simulated stand-ins in a second, deeper layer of each person, their dispositions and means: how much risk they are willing to take, how they act under stress, how they came through past disruptions and how much room their work and money leave them to adapt, gathered with their consent, for example through surveys.

The warning that does exist points in an unexpected direction. Every tree, whichever sample it was grown on, asks first about regularity, and the more regular people were, the more often their routine broke: three in ten of the most regular fifth against one in seven of the least regular. Regular commuters, people whose daytime place is busiest at midday, and people who travel to a daytime place away from home were most at risk. The tree draws that last line close to home, at about 600 m, and the regular people it flags typically travel about 7.5 km. What these people share is a day built around one fixed trip to one fixed place.

Simple trees did better than complex ones. The fully grown tree memorised its training people and failed on new ones, pruning improved it step by step, and the best tree stops at 54 leaves. Many different trees fit the data almost equally well, so the exact cut on regularity matters less than the fact that regularity is always the first question. And people who looked alike on every measure still went different ways, which is why the signs can say where to look but not who will need help.

For the project this is a central result. A simulated stand-in looks most reliable for the people it describes best in ordinary times, those with regular weeks, and those are exactly the people whose stand-ins failed most often in the emergency. How good a stand-in looks before a disruption is not a guide to how well it will hold up during one. The result also answers the question the NaiveBayes tab left open, whether the failures could have been foreseen: only in part, but well enough to set an order. A system that keeps a budget for refreshing its stand-ins should spend it first on people with a fixed daily trip, and it should decide openly how many false alarms it will accept for every person it does not want to miss. It should treat the signs as a starting order, not a verdict, and keep watching every person against their own ordinary weeks, because the signs and the alarm both see regular lives best.

Three directions for further work follow. The first is the deeper layer of dispositions and means. The second is the places around a person's home. The trees counted only how many places of any kind each home square holds, and that count carried little weight. But YJMob100K counts 85 kinds of places in every square, still unnamed, and if having shops, clinics or schools within walking distance helps a routine hold in an emergency, city planning would gain a measurable reason to keep everyday services close to where people live. Reading what those kinds of places are is the first study on the [Research](../research/#reading-anonymous-places-through-named-ones) tab. The third is the other half of this tab's result: the people with irregular weeks whose routines also broke, whom these signs and the alarm mostly miss, and whom the [Research](../research/#invisible-breaks) tab follows.
