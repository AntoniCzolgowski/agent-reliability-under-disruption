<p class="lede">Without context, a day of the emergency looks much like an ordinary day. Naive Bayes, a simple classifier, picks up the difference from where and when each person was seen, half hour by half hour. Read person by person, the same method lets a city watch a shock spread through daily life one person at a time: when it reached each of them, whom it reached first, and whose change a tool built for the average person would miss.</p>

## Overview

Naive Bayes sorts things into classes by weighing many small pieces of evidence. It is a supervised method: it learns from examples whose class is already known and then predicts the class of new ones. The classic use is a spam filter. From thousands of emails already marked as spam or not spam, it learns how often each word appears in each class. A new email is then read word by word. Words that are common in spam push the verdict toward spam, words that are common in ordinary mail push it the other way, and the class with the stronger total wins. The same idea sorts news articles by topic, marks product reviews as positive or negative and gives a first guess at a diagnosis from a list of symptoms. It is fast, needs little data and is easy to inspect, so it is often the first model tried on a new problem.

The name comes from Bayes' rule, which turns "how likely is this evidence if the class is true" into "how likely is the class given this evidence". For an example made of the words w1, w2 and so on up to wn, the probability of a class c is proportional to `P(c) × P(w1 | c) × P(w2 | c) × … × P(wn | c)`. Here P(c) is the prior, the share of training examples that belong to class c, and each P(w | c) is how common the word w is in class c. The method is called naive because multiplying the words' probabilities assumes that, inside a class, words appear independently of each other. That is rarely true: in an email, "free" and "offer" tend to come together. The assumption is still useful, because it turns an impossible task, estimating the probability of every possible combination of words, into a simple one, counting each word on its own.

**Multinomial Naive Bayes** is the version for counts. Each example is described by how many times each word of a fixed vocabulary appears in it. Training is counting: for each class, add up how often each word appears across all training examples of that class and divide by the total number of words in that class, which gives P(word | class). Prediction is adding up: for a new example, start from the logarithm of the prior and add the logarithm of P(word | class) once for every word it contains. Logarithms turn the long product into a sum and keep very small numbers from vanishing. The class with the largest total is the prediction, and the totals can be turned back into a probability for each class. Figure 1 shows the idea on a day, with each half hour as a word.

Counting has one trap. If a word never appears in one class during training, its probability in that class is zero, and since the probabilities are multiplied, a single zero makes the whole product zero. Any new example that contains the word could then never belong to that class, however strongly every other word points to it (Figure 2). **Smoothing** prevents this by adding a small number, alpha, to every count before dividing: P(word | class) becomes (count + alpha) divided by (total + alpha times the size of the vocabulary). With alpha = 1 this is called Laplace smoothing. A word never seen in a class gets a small probability instead of none, and rare words are pulled a little toward the average. Alpha is chosen on data that the model was not trained on, here the validation people described below.

<Figure n={2} title="Why Naive Bayes needs smoothing" src={o07} alt="Two panels with toy counts of four words. Left: bars for ordinary and emergency training days; the word place 03:00 was written 3 times on ordinary days and 0 times on emergency days, marked with a red 0. Right: the probability of each word on emergency days, a red line without smoothing that drops to exactly 0 for place 03:00, and a green line with alpha equal to 1 that gives it a small probability, with an arrow labelled: probability 0, any day with this word would be ruled out as an emergency day.">
A word that never appeared on emergency training days gets probability 0 without smoothing (red), which would rule out the emergency for every day that contains it. Adding alpha = 1 to every count (green) gives that word a small chance and leaves the other probabilities almost unchanged.
</Figure>

**Bernoulli Naive Bayes** is the version for yes-or-no features. Each example is a list of switches, one per feature, and each switch is either on (1) or off (0). Training estimates, for each class, how often each switch is on. Prediction multiplies, feature by feature, the probability of what was observed: the chance that a switch is on where it is on, and the chance that it is off where it is off. The difference from the multinomial model is that an absent feature counts as evidence too. In a spam filter, a Bernoulli model notices that an email does not contain the word "unsubscribe", while a multinomial model simply skips the words that are not there. Figure 3 shows the two ways of reading the same person-day: the multinomial model reads where the person was in the half hours in which they were seen, and the Bernoulli model reads only whether they were seen in each of the 47 half hours.

<Figure n={3} title="One person-day, two ways of reading it" src={o08} alt="Two strips of 47 squares, one per half hour from 00:30 to 23:30, for person 14892 on workday 58. Top strip: 27 coloured squares with letters, H for home in blue around 06:00 and after 21:00, E for elsewhere in yellow in the morning and late afternoon, P for the daytime place in green through the middle of the day, and 20 empty white squares. Bottom strip: the same 47 squares as dark squares marked 1 where the person was seen and white squares marked 0 where they were not.">
The same day of one person as the multinomial model reads it (top: 27 words, one per seen half hour) and as the Bernoulli model reads it (bottom: 47 switches, seen or not seen). The multinomial model learns from where the person was, the Bernoulli model from when they were seen at all.
</Figure>

In this project the examples are **person-days**: one day in the life of one person. YJMob100K follows 25,000 anonymous people in one city for 75 days and records which 500 m square of a grid each of them is in, in half-hour steps; days 60 to 74 are an emergency (see the [Introduction](../introduction/) and [DataPrep_EDA](../dataprep_eda/) tabs). The two classes are the days of an ordinary fortnight and the days of the emergency. If a model can tell them apart, the emergency changed what people's days looked like. If it can tell them apart for some people and not for others, that shows whose days changed. Three Naive Bayes models are compared: the standard multinomial model, a Bernoulli model, and a third model, written from scratch, that reads only where people were. Naive Bayes is then extended into a detector that knows each person's own habits, which gives a day-by-day picture of when each person's **routine**, their usual pattern of where they are at each time of day, broke. Whose routine broke becomes the label that the [DecTrees](../dectrees/) tab tries to predict from ordinary days alone.

## Data

Naive Bayes is supervised, so it needs labelled data: examples whose class is known in advance. Here the label comes from the calendar, not from anyone's judgement. Days 45 to 59 are an ordinary fortnight and get the label "ordinary". Days 60 to 74 are the emergency and get the label "emergency". Both fortnights hold the same mix of days: ten workdays, four weekend-like days and one holiday-like day (the DataPrep_EDA tab explains how the days were typed). Days 0 to 44 never enter training; they are kept for learning each person's habits later on this tab.

The data starts as the cleaned record table of the DataPrep_EDA tab (Table 1). Each row is one record saying that a person was seen in a grid square during a half hour of a day, 28,956,057 rows for the 24,514 people who have a kind of mover on the [Clustering](../clustering/) tab. Two places are fixed for every person from days 0 to 44, the same way as on the Clustering tab. **Home** is the square where they are seen most often between midnight and 5 am. The **daytime place** is the square where they are seen most often between 10 am and 4 pm on workdays, which for many people is a workplace or a school. Each place counts as the 3 by 3 block of squares around it, about 1.5 km across, because a person who stays in one spot is often recorded in a neighbouring square as well.

<Figure label="Table" n={1} title="Before: the cleaned record table" src={t02} small alt="Table image with eight rows for person 1 and the columns of the cleaned YJMob100K table: uid, d, t, x, y, phase, weekend, holiday_like, window, gap_prev, step_prev, adjacent, big_jump, flicker_mid, split and eligible.">
The input as it comes out of the DataPrep_EDA tab: one row per record, with the person, the day, the half hour and the grid square. Nothing in it is a day of a person yet.
</Figure>

Every person-day then becomes a row of 47 letters, one for each half hour from 00:30 to 23:30 (Table 2). The letter is H if the person was seen at home, P if they were seen at their daytime place, E if they were seen anywhere else, and a dot if they were not seen at all. Most half hours are dots, because a phone is recorded only now and then: 32.8% of all person-half-hours are seen, and of those 34% are at home, 14% at the daytime place and 52% elsewhere. A day counts as readable if at least four of its half hours are seen, and 677,295 of the 735,420 person-days in the two fortnights are readable (92%).

Six labelled days of six development people, three from the ordinary fortnight and three from the emergency. Each row is one day of one person, written as 47 half hours: H home, P daytime place, E elsewhere, and a dot for not seen.
</Figure>

One half hour was left out. Midnight (00:00) is recorded much less often on every emergency day than on ordinary days: it keeps only 70% of its ordinary share of records, while every other half hour keeps between 83% and 95% (Figure 4). That looks like a quirk of how the data were recorded rather than of how people moved, and a model given that half hour learns the quirk. A Bernoulli model that sees nothing but midnight already labels 53.1% of the test days correctly. So 00:00 is dropped, and every day has 47 half hours.

Scikit-learn's multinomial Naive Bayes needs numbers, so each day is turned into counts of words. A word joins the type of day, the half hour and the place, for example "work 08:30 elsewhere" or "rest 22:00 home". With 2 types of day, 47 half hours and 3 places there are 282 possible words, and each seen half hour of a day writes exactly one of them. A day becomes a row of 282 counts, most of them zero (Table 3). The Bernoulli model reads a simpler row of 47 switches, seen or not seen. The third model, written from scratch, reads the letters directly.

The same six days as counts of the 282 words that multinomial Naive Bayes reads, with nine of the columns shown. A 1 means the person was seen in that half hour at that place, and a row adds up to the number of half hours the person was seen that day.

The data are split into training and test sets **by person**, not by day. On the DataPrep_EDA tab every person was assigned once, at random, to a development group (70%) or an evaluation group (30%), and the evaluation people have not been used to fit anything since. Of the 24,514 people here, 17,164 are development people and 7,350 evaluation people. Here the development people are split again: 80% of them train the models, and the other 20% are the validation set, used only to choose the smoothing alpha. The evaluation people are the test set (Table 4). All the days of one person go to the same set, and nobody appears in two sets.

The split has to be disjoint because a test set exists to show how well a model works on data it has never seen. If the same days, or even other days of the same people, were in both sets, a model could do well by recognising those particular people's habits instead of the difference between ordinary and emergency days, and the test score would promise more than the model can deliver for anybody new. Splitting by person also matches how a result like this would be used, on people the model was not built from. Table 2 shows six training days and Table 5 six test days. Both sets hold almost exactly the same share of emergency days (50.9% and 51.0%), so a model that always answered "emergency" would be right on 51.0% of the test days, the bar that every model below has to clear.

## Results

### Can a model tell an emergency day from an ordinary one?

Three measures are used. **Accuracy** is the share of test days a model labels correctly. A **confusion matrix** splits that into four counts: ordinary days called ordinary, ordinary days called emergency, emergency days called ordinary, and emergency days called emergency. **AUC** asks a slightly different question: if one emergency day and one ordinary day are picked at random, how often does the model give the emergency day the higher probability? An AUC of 0.5 is a coin flip and 1 is perfect. AUC is used across this site because it does not depend on where the line between the two answers is drawn.

All three models do only a little better than a coin (Figure 5). On the 202,744 test days, multinomial Naive Bayes is right 52.7% of the time (AUC 0.538), the model that reads only where people were 53.1% (AUC 0.532), and the Bernoulli model, which reads only when people were seen, 57.5% (AUC 0.591). Always answering "emergency" would already score 51.0%. The confusion matrices show how the models go wrong. The multinomial model calls about half of each kind of day an emergency. The where-only model calls 65% of the ordinary days emergency days, so it finds 70% of the real emergency days mostly by suspecting most days.

<Figure n={5} title="Confusion matrices and accuracy on the test days" src={f35} alt="Three confusion matrices of the test days with actual class in rows and predicted class in columns. Multinomial, accuracy 52.7 percent: 50,822 ordinary days called ordinary, 48,471 called emergency, 47,355 emergency days called ordinary, 56,096 called emergency. Bernoulli, accuracy 57.5 percent: 52,279, 47,014, 39,245 and 64,206. Where only, accuracy 53.1 percent: 34,931, 64,362, 30,826 and 72,625.">
The three Naive Bayes models on the 202,744 test days, with the count and the row share in each cell. All three sit close to the 51.0% of always answering "emergency", and the Bernoulli model, which only reads when people were seen, does best.
</Figure>

The Bernoulli result needs a second look. The number of seen half hours alone, without any model, separates the two fortnights with an AUC of 0.588, almost as well as the Bernoulli model's 0.591. **So most of what the best model knows is that people were recorded less, or at different times, during the emergency, not where they went.** Part of that difference lies in the recording rather than in people's lives, as midnight showed in Figure 4. The where-only model ignores how often people were seen and reads only where they were, and it gets the least out of the data. That matches what the DataPrep_EDA tab found: in the emergency people were recorded less, but the shape of an average day barely changed.

### Smoothing hardly matters here, overconfidence does

Smoothing was chosen on the validation people, trying alpha from 0.0001 to 10 (Figure 6). Validation accuracy does not move at all. With 380,000 training days, every one of the 282 words appears many times in both classes, so adding at most 10 to each count changes nothing. Alpha = 10 gave the lowest log loss and was kept for all three models. Smoothing does matter one level down: the 45 ordinary days of a single person leave 48.4% of their own combinations of type of day, half hour and place empty, so a detector built for one person needs it, and the next section uses it there.

The right half of Figure 6 shows a weakness that smoothing cannot fix. **Log loss** measures how confident and how wrong a model's probabilities are, and a model that always says 50% scores 0.693. All three models score worse than that, between 0.77 and 0.86, so they are overconfident. The reason is the naive assumption. The half hours of one day are not independent: a person at home at 07:30 is very likely to be at home at 08:00 too, but the model counts the second half hour as new evidence, and twenty such half hours make it far more certain than the data allow. The models rank days a little better than chance, but their probabilities should not be read as real chances.

Left: validation accuracy, three flat lines at about 56.8 percent for Bernoulli, 52.9 percent for where only and 52.6 percent for multinomial. Right: validation log loss, three flat lines at about 0.86, 0.78 and 0.77, all above a dashed line at 0.693 labelled a coin flip.">

### One detector for everyone, or one for each person?

The where-only model learned one picture of an ordinary day for everybody. Many people's own ordinary days do not look like that picture, and the model takes their normal life for an emergency. On ordinary test days it calls 65% of the days emergency days overall, 87% for weekend movers, 80% for far roamers and 78% for homebodies, but only 33% for regular commuters, whose days are closest to the average picture (Figure 7, right).

The fix is **empirical Bayes**: a detector for each person that borrows strength from similar people. A single person's 45 ordinary days are too few to estimate their habits for every half hour on their own. So each person's habits are pulled toward the habits of their kind of mover, and each kind's habits toward everyone's. The pull works like smoothing with a prior. A person's share of a place at a given half hour is their own count plus m times their kind's share, divided by their own number of seen half hours plus m. With few records the kind's habits dominate, and with many the person's own do. The strength m is chosen by the data, as the value under which the people's actual days are most probable (Figure 7, left). It comes out at m = 4 pseudo-observations per half hour for each person, and between 4 and 64 for the kinds.

Per-person detectors cut the false alarms on ordinary days from 65% to 52% overall and spread them much more evenly. Weekend movers fall from 87% to 40%, homebodies from 78% to 47% and far roamers from 80% to 54%. Regular commuters rise from 33% to 45%, because the one-size detector had been easy on them. AUC improves a little, from 0.532 for one detector for everyone to 0.548 for one per kind and 0.559 for one per person. The gain in fairness is larger than the gain in accuracy: a detector that knows whose day it is reading treats unusual people's ordinary lives much more like everyone else's.

<Figure n={7} title="One detector for everyone, one per kind, one per person" src={f36} alt="Two panels. Left: log marginal likelihood against prior strength m on a log scale from 0.25 to 64, a curve that peaks at m equal to 4, marked by a dashed line. Right: grouped bars for the six kinds of movers showing the percentage of ordinary days called an emergency by one detector for everyone in red, one per kind in yellow and one per person in green; the red bars reach 80 percent for far roamers, 87 percent for weekend movers and 78 percent for homebodies but 33 percent for regular commuters, while the yellow and green bars stay between 40 and 59 percent around a dashed line at 50.">
Left: how probable the people's own days are under each prior strength m, highest at m = 4. Right: the share of ordinary test days that each detector calls an emergency, by kind of mover; one detector for everyone (red) is wrong most often for the people least like the average.
</Figure>

This matters well beyond the model. A tool tuned to the average resident does not treat residents equally. With one picture of an ordinary day for everybody, the same detector raised false alarms on 87% of weekend movers' ordinary days and on 33% of commuters'. Any emergency measure calibrated on the average person, such as one threshold for who gets a welfare check or one estimate of who will keep commuting, carries the same tilt: it is wrong most often for the people whose lives are least like the average. The remedy shown here is to build from the individual up. Each person is judged against their own ordinary days, and where their own record is thin, it is filled in from people like them. Groups still help: one detector per kind of mover already removes most of the tilt (false alarms on 53% of ordinary days, against 52% with one per person), so kinds of movers make a good scaffold. But the unit of a decision has to be the person. For emergency planning this means modelling people one by one and adding them up to the city, rather than starting from an average and applying it to everyone.

### When each routine broke

The per-person detector can read every day of a person against that person's own habits and give it a probability of being an emergency day. A day in days 0 to 44 is read with habits learned from the other two of the three ordinary fortnights, so no day is judged by habits learned from itself. Averaged over the evaluation people of each kind, these probabilities give a day-by-day curve of when routines changed (Figure 8, workdays only).

Before day 60 the curves hover around 50%. On day 60 they step up, for all evaluation people from 50.7% on day 59 to 54.7% on day 60, and they stay up until day 74. Averaged over the emergency, regular commuters' days step up the furthest, to 59.3%, followed by weekend movers (55.5%) and local regulars (54.1%), while far roamers (51.8%) and homebodies (52.0%) barely move. The detector knows nothing about the calendar, so the step on day 60 comes from the days themselves. One ordinary day also jumps: day 36, the workday just before the holiday-like day 37, rises to 56% for weekend movers and 55% for regular commuters. Something made that day unusual, and the data do not say what. **It is a reminder that unusual days happen without an emergency, and that any alarm has to be judged against them.**

### Whose habit model broke

A probability for each day still does not say whether a person's routine broke over the whole fortnight. For that, each person gets a **habit model**: for every half hour of a workday and of a rest day, the share of their seen half hours spent at home, at their daytime place and elsewhere during days 0 to 44, pulled toward their kind as above. It is the simplest possible agent of a person, a description of them that predicts where they will be. Its **surprise** at a seen half hour is how unlikely it found what the person actually did, measured as minus the logarithm of the probability it gave. A person's routine **broke** if their average surprise rose from the ordinary fortnight (days 45 to 59) to the emergency (days 60 to 74) by more than chance allows.

Chance is measured with a placebo: the same comparison between two ordinary fortnights, days 30 to 44 against days 45 to 59, with habits from days 0 to 29, so the gap between the habits and the tested days is the same. Nothing special happened between those fortnights, so whatever rise the placebo shows is ordinary wobble. A routine counts as broken if its rise in surprise is above the line that only 5% of placebo rises cross. The line is drawn separately for people who are seen more or less often, because few records make the rise noisier (Figure 9).

Of the 23,904 people with at least 30 seen half hours in every fortnight compared, 19.1% broke, against the 5.0% that the placebo lets through by design. **So the emergency broke about one routine in five, four times as many as chance would.** It broke the routines of regular commuters most often (28.8%), then of local regulars (21.0%), homebodies (20.9%) and weekend movers (19.2%), and those of far roamers (15.6%) and mid-range movers (13.8%) least. The share is almost the same for people who are seen rarely and people who are seen often (from 18.0% to 20.6% across five groups), so the label is not just a measure of how well someone is recorded. This label, broke or held, is what the [DecTrees](../dectrees/) tab tries to predict from ordinary days alone.

### An alarm that adds up the evidence

The label is known only once the fortnight is over, and a city would want to know sooner. An **alarm** can be built from the per-person detector. Each day adds its evidence for the emergency, minus a small allowance, to a running total that never goes below zero, and the alarm fires when the total passes a line. **This is a CUSUM, short for cumulative sum, a standard tool for noticing when a process has changed.** The allowance stops ordinary day-to-day wobble from piling up, and the line is set so that the alarm fires for only 5% of people on the ordinary days 15 to 44.

Among the evaluation people, the alarm fires for 35.2% of those whose routine broke and for 9.3% of those whose routine held, typically on day 64, **four days into the emergency** (Figure 10). It is very uneven across kinds of movers: it fires for 44.1% of regular commuters but for 1.3% of far roamers and 3.1% of homebodies. The [Research](../research/#invisible-breaks) tab follows this up, because the people the alarm misses are not the people whose routines held. Many of their routines broke too, and the alarm simply cannot see it.

CUSUM is a trusted tool with a narrow view. E. S. Page designed it to catch a production line drifting out of control ([Page, 1954](https://doi.org/10.1093/biomet/41.1-2.100)), the CDC's Early Aberration Reporting System runs three versions of it on daily counts of symptoms to spot outbreaks ([Zhu et al., 2005](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/preview/mmwrhtml/su5401a20.htm)), and hospitals use it to watch surgical outcomes. Each of these watches one stream against one fixed idea of normal. The alarm on this tab watches every person against that person's own normal, learned from their ordinary weeks and filled in from similar people where their records are thin. It works on sparse, irregular records, and its false-alarm rate is fixed in advance. With people's consent, that opens uses an aggregate chart cannot serve: reaching, within days, the people whose routine a crisis has just broken, for instance older people living alone who have agreed to be checked on; noticing when patients sent home from hospital stop keeping their usual day; or telling a transit agency whose commute has changed, not only that ridership fell. The same design fits anything with a routine, people or not: each machine on a factory floor, each household's electricity use, each device on a network, each judged against its own ordinary rhythm.

### A check by a pretrained forecaster

Naive Bayes reads days one at a time. Chronos-2, a forecasting model pretrained by Amazon on a very large collection of other time series, reads a person's whole history instead. With no training on this data at all, it forecast each person's daily share of seen half hours at home and at the daytime place for 15 days ahead, once from day 44 and once from day 59, and its forecast error measures how far each day departed from that person's own past. The error separates emergency days from ordinary days with an AUC of 0.601, better than any Naive Bayes model on this tab (Figure 11). It grows in the emergency for 67.9% of people, most of all for regular commuters, and a person's rise in error agrees with the habit-model label above: it tells who broke from who held with an AUC of 0.777. Two very different methods, one counting words and one pretrained on other data, **point to the same people.**

## Conclusions

A single day of the emergency looks a lot like an ordinary day. Read half hour by half hour, the best Naive Bayes model tells the two apart only a little more often than a coin would, and most of what it finds is that people were recorded less, not that they went somewhere else. For most people, life during the emergency kept its usual shape: home at night, out during the day and back in the evening.

Whose day it is matters more than any single day. A detector that learned one picture of an ordinary day for everybody mistakes many people's normal lives for an emergency, most of all for people who live least like the average, such as weekend movers, homebodies and people who range widely across the city. A detector that knows each person's own habits, filled in where needed from people like them, is fairer and a little more accurate. For anyone planning for an emergency, the lesson is to model people one by one and add them up, not to start from the average and apply it to everyone.

Read that way, routines did break. Over the emergency fortnight, about one person in five stopped matching the habits of their ordinary weeks, four times as many as between two ordinary fortnights, and the change began on the first day of the emergency. Regular commuters, the people with the most predictable weeks, changed most often. An alarm that adds up the evidence day by day catches a third of these breaks within days, most of them among people with regular weeks.

For anyone building decision systems on simulated people, this tab gives three working rules. First, plan for failure: when daily life is disrupted, about one simulated stand-in in five stops describing its person, and the failures concentrate among people whose weeks were the most regular, so a system needs a budget for refreshing stand-ins, not only for building them. Second, monitor every stand-in against its own person's ordinary days rather than against the average resident, so that the system's errors fall evenly across the population. Third, treat early warning as something measured: an alarm set on ordinary days catches about a third of the failures, typically four days in, and the people it cannot see need a different detector, not more patience. The [DecTrees](../dectrees/) tab tests whether the failures could have been predicted before the emergency began, and the [Research](../research/#invisible-breaks) tab measures the alarm's blind spot and tests a fairer design.
